Every operational change is a bet.

You ship it, and then you find out.

What it is

Your operations already write an append-only history. We run it again.

Simulacro forks that history at any moment, replays it forward with one policy changed, and scores the result against what actually happened. One arm of the comparison is not a simulation — it is your own past. That is the whole reason to believe the other arm.

The receipt

Every answer states how much of it was real.

Most simulators produce confident numbers about a world that does not exist and offer no way to check them. Here reproducibility is carried per branch and withdrawn automatically the moment anything unrepeatable enters the run.

What you get

Three things, and the first one needs no simulation at all.

Day one

Catch it before it lands

Declare what must never be true — stock below zero, a fulfilment without a payment, a promise you cannot keep — and the governor refuses the action rather than logging the damage afterwards.

Once there is history

Ask what would have happened

Replay a real week with the pricing rule changed, the escalation threshold moved, the refund policy rewritten. Compare against the week you actually had.

Then

Let it find the change

State an objective and a set of constraints. Simulacro proposes operating policies, evaluates them against your own history, and returns the ones that survive.

The obvious objections

Reasonable things to distrust about this.

It is still a simulation. Why would I trust the number?

Because the baseline is not simulated. Every counterfactual is scored against the recorded outcome of the same period, so a replay that cannot reproduce what already happened is visibly wrong before it is ever used to predict.

Is this a language model guessing at my numbers?

No model computes the ledger and no model judges whether a constraint held. Both are code. Models propose actions and read messy inputs into typed facts; everything downstream of that is arithmetic you can audit.

We already A/B test.

A/B testing spends real customers and real weeks, and it cannot evaluate a change you only make once. This costs a replay and returns in minutes — and the two compose: use it to decide what is worth testing.

Our operational history is a mess.

Then start at day one, where the value needs no history: declare the constraints, run the governor, and the log accumulates as a side effect of the thing already paying for itself.
